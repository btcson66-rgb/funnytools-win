# How Do You Convert a Percentage to GPA? Why 85% Is Not Always 3.4

The tempting calculation is simple: `percentage ÷ 100 × 4`. It is also often the wrong answer for an application, transcript review, or scholarship form. A GPA is produced by a grading policy: percentages may first become letter grades, letter grades map to grade points, and course credits may then weight the points.

> **Short answer:** there is no worldwide percentage-to-GPA conversion table. Use the receiving institution's instructions, an official transcript evaluation, or the scale printed by your school. Treat a calculator estimate as a planning aid, not as an official academic record.

## Why a linear conversion fails

Percentage marks and grade points measure different things. A percentage is usually tied to an assessment rubric. A GPA scale groups a range of marks into letter-grade bands such as A, B, or C. The width and boundaries of those bands vary by school, course, country, and purpose.

Consider an 85% mark. Under a linear estimate it becomes 3.40 on a 4.0 scale. Under a policy that places 85–89 in an A band worth 4.0, it becomes 4.0. Under another policy it may be A−, worth 3.7, or B+, worth 3.3. None of these is automatically “the” answer because the scale is part of the question.

The same warning applies to 4.3 scales, plus/minus grades, pass/fail courses, repeated courses, and honors weighting. A number without its scale is incomplete information.

## The three layers to identify

1. **Original mark.** Is it a percentage, a letter grade, a rank, or a result already reported on a GPA scale?
2. **Grade-band table.** Which percentage interval maps to which letter grade at the issuing school or receiving institution?
3. **Grade-point and credit rule.** How many points does each letter carry, and are courses weighted by credits, contact hours, or another rule?

If any layer is missing, label the result as an estimate. Do not reverse-engineer an official GPA from a single percentage when the policy is not available.

## A defensible workflow

### For an application

Read the application field and its help text first. Some forms ask for the GPA exactly as printed; others ask for the original average, grading scale, or a credential evaluation. If the form requests an international conversion, use the named evaluator or the institution's own instructions. Attach the transcript when the process allows it.

### For comparing study plans

You can use [GPA calculator](/en/tools/gpa-calculator/) or [weighted average calculator](/en/tools/weighted-average-calculator/) to model a known scale. Enter each course's grade point and credit value, not a guessed percentage conversion. Record the scale beside the result, for example “3.62 / 4.00, school table, 18 credits”.

### For a rough personal estimate

If no policy is available, show the assumption openly: “85% treated as 3.4 by linear 4.0 estimate; unofficial.” Run a sensitivity check with two plausible bands (for example 3.3 and 3.7) so a decision is not built on false precision.

## Worked credit-weighted example

Suppose a school has already supplied these grade points: a 3-credit course at 3.7, a 4-credit course at 3.3, and a 2-credit course at 4.0. The credit-weighted GPA is:

`(3 × 3.7 + 4 × 3.3 + 2 × 4.0) ÷ (3 + 4 + 2) = 32.3 ÷ 9 = 3.59`

This calculation is reproducible because the scale and credit values are known. Replacing those points with `percentage ÷ 100 × 4` would introduce a separate, undocumented assumption for every course.

## Common mistakes

- Calling a linear estimate “official GPA”.
- Mixing 4.0 and 4.3 points in the same average.
- Averaging course GPAs without weighting their credits when the policy requires credit weighting.
- Converting a pass/fail course into a numeric point without a published rule.
- Ignoring repeated-course, honors, withdrawal, or transfer-credit treatment.
- Copying a conversion table from a different institution or application cycle.

Keep the original transcript and the source of any table you use. The [GPA calculator](/en/tools/gpa-calculator/) and [weighted average calculator](/en/tools/weighted-average-calculator/) can model the arithmetic once the scale and credits are known.

## Reliable references and next steps

When a formal submission is involved, prefer the registrar, admissions office, application instructions, or a named credential evaluator. A general explanation from [World Education Services](https://www.wes.org/advisor-blog/gpa-calculator/) can help you understand why scales differ, but it does not replace the target institution's rule.

Write down the original percentage, the issuing scale, the conversion source, and whether the result is official or estimated. That small audit trail prevents a plausible-looking number from being mistaken for a transcript fact.

## What to do when the instructions are ambiguous

Ask the registrar or admissions office one focused question: “Should I report the GPA printed on my transcript, the original percentage, or a conversion supplied by an evaluator?” Include the grading scale and country, but do not send a full transcript to an unverified contact address. If the deadline arrives before you receive a reply, enter the original value when the form permits it and label any calculated figure as unofficial in an additional-information field. Keep a screenshot or PDF of the instruction page and the date you asked. A transparent assumption is easier for a reviewer to correct than an unexplained 3.4 that looks official.
